MEDICAL FAQ - NUTRITION AND DIET

Q: What is a balanced diet?
A: A balanced diet includes a variety of foods in the right proportions to provide the nutrients necessary for good health. It includes fruits and vegetables (at least 5 portions per day), starchy carbohydrates (preferably whole grain), proteins (lean meat, fish, eggs, beans, legumes), dairy or dairy alternatives, and small amounts of healthy fats. A balanced diet provides adequate calories, macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, fats), and micronutrients (vitamins, minerals) while limiting processed foods, sugar, and excessive sodium.

Q: What are macronutrients?
A: Macronutrients are nutrients required in large amounts that provide energy. The three main macronutrients are carbohydrates (4 calories per gram), proteins (4 calories per gram), and fats (9 calories per gram). Carbohydrates are the body's primary energy source. Proteins are essential for building and repairing tissues. Fats support cell function, hormone production, and vitamin absorption. Water is sometimes called the fourth macronutrient as it is needed in large quantities.

Q: What vitamins and minerals are most important?
A: Key vitamins include Vitamin A (vision, immune function), B vitamins (energy metabolism, nerve function), Vitamin C (immune system, collagen synthesis), Vitamin D (calcium absorption, bone health), Vitamin E (antioxidant), and Vitamin K (blood clotting). Important minerals include calcium (bones and teeth), iron (oxygen transport), magnesium (muscle and nerve function), potassium (blood pressure regulation), zinc (immune function), and iodine (thyroid function). Deficiencies in these can cause serious health problems.

Q: How many calories does a person need per day?
A: Caloric needs vary based on age, sex, height, weight, and activity level. On average, adult women need about 1,600-2,400 calories per day and adult men need about 2,000-3,000 calories per day. Active individuals need more calories. The Dietary Reference Intake (DRI) provides specific recommendations. Online calculators using the Harris-Benedict or Mifflin-St Jeor equation can estimate individual caloric needs more precisely based on personal characteristics and activity level.

Q: What is the Mediterranean diet?
A: The Mediterranean diet is based on the traditional eating habits of people in countries bordering the Mediterranean Sea. It emphasizes plant foods (vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, whole grains), olive oil as the primary fat, moderate amounts of fish and seafood, low to moderate amounts of dairy and eggs, and limited red meat and processed foods. It is associated with reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, and cognitive decline, and is consistently ranked among the healthiest diets in the world.

Q: How much water should a person drink per day?
A: The National Academies of Sciences recommends about 3.7 liters (125 oz) per day for men and 2.7 liters (91 oz) for women total water intake, including water from all beverages and foods. Plain water, while the best hydrator, is not the only source — fruits, vegetables, and other beverages contribute. Water needs increase with physical activity, heat, illness, and pregnancy or breastfeeding. Thirst is a general indicator, but certain populations (elderly, athletes) should monitor intake more carefully.

Q: What is the glycemic index?
A: The glycemic index (GI) is a rating system for foods containing carbohydrates that shows how quickly each food affects blood sugar levels. Foods are rated on a scale of 0 to 100. Low GI foods (below 55) such as legumes and most fruits cause a slow rise in blood sugar. High GI foods (above 70) such as white bread and sugary drinks cause rapid spikes. For people with diabetes or metabolic conditions, choosing low GI foods can help maintain stable blood sugar and improve overall health.

Q: What are antioxidants and why are they important?
A: Antioxidants are substances that protect cells from damage caused by free radicals — unstable molecules produced during metabolism and in response to environmental factors like pollution and UV radiation. Key dietary antioxidants include vitamins C and E, beta-carotene, selenium, and various phytochemicals found in colorful fruits and vegetables (like lycopene in tomatoes and anthocyanins in berries). A diet rich in antioxidants is associated with reduced risk of chronic diseases including heart disease, cancer, and age-related conditions.

Q: Is skipping breakfast harmful?
A: Research on breakfast and health outcomes is mixed. Traditionally, breakfast was considered the most important meal of the day for metabolism and focus. However, recent studies suggest that the quality of overall diet matters more than whether breakfast is eaten. Intermittent fasting, which often involves skipping breakfast, can be beneficial for some people. Children and adolescents generally benefit from eating breakfast for concentration and academic performance. Individuals with diabetes should consult their doctor about breakfast timing and content.

Q: What is fiber and why is it important?
A: Dietary fiber is a type of carbohydrate found in plant foods that the body cannot fully digest. Soluble fiber (found in oats, beans, apples) dissolves in water and helps lower blood cholesterol and glucose levels. Insoluble fiber (found in whole grains, nuts, vegetables) adds bulk to stool and aids digestion. Adults should consume 25-38 grams of fiber per day. A high-fiber diet is associated with reduced risk of heart disease, diabetes, colorectal cancer, and obesity, and promotes healthy gut microbiome.
