MEDICAL FAQ - HYPERTENSION (HIGH BLOOD PRESSURE)

Q: What is hypertension?
A: Hypertension, commonly known as high blood pressure, is a condition in which the force of blood against artery walls is consistently too high. Blood pressure is measured in millimeters of mercury (mmHg) and recorded as two numbers: systolic pressure (when the heart beats) over diastolic pressure (when the heart rests). A reading of 130/80 mmHg or higher is considered hypertension by most medical guidelines.

Q: What causes high blood pressure?
A: Primary (essential) hypertension has no identifiable cause and develops gradually over many years. Secondary hypertension is caused by an underlying condition such as kidney disease, adrenal gland tumors, thyroid problems, or use of certain medications. Risk factors include age, family history, obesity, sedentary lifestyle, excessive salt intake, heavy alcohol use, stress, and smoking.

Q: What are the symptoms of hypertension?
A: Hypertension is often called the "silent killer" because most people with high blood pressure have no symptoms. In severe cases, symptoms may include severe headaches, nosebleeds, shortness of breath, chest pain, dizziness, and visual changes. The only reliable way to know if you have hypertension is to have your blood pressure measured regularly.

Q: How is hypertension diagnosed?
A: Hypertension is diagnosed through blood pressure measurements taken with a sphygmomanometer. A diagnosis requires consistently elevated readings on multiple occasions. Doctors typically confirm diagnosis after 2-3 elevated readings on separate visits. Home blood pressure monitoring and 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring may also be used for more accurate diagnosis.

Q: How is hypertension treated?
A: Treatment includes lifestyle modifications and medications. Lifestyle changes include adopting a heart-healthy diet (DASH diet), reducing sodium intake, increasing physical activity, maintaining healthy weight, limiting alcohol, and quitting smoking. Medications include diuretics, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), calcium channel blockers, and beta-blockers. Often a combination is needed for adequate control.

Q: What is the DASH diet?
A: The DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) diet is an eating plan specifically designed to help lower blood pressure. It emphasizes fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins (poultry, fish, nuts), and low-fat dairy products. It limits foods high in saturated fats, cholesterol, and sodium, as well as sweets and sugary beverages. Following the DASH diet can reduce blood pressure by 8-14 mmHg.

Q: What complications can hypertension cause?
A: Uncontrolled high blood pressure damages blood vessels and organs over time. Complications include heart attack, heart failure, stroke, aneurysm, kidney failure, and vision loss. It is a leading risk factor for cardiovascular disease globally. Hypertension can also cause cognitive decline and dementia. Consistent treatment and management are critical to preventing these serious outcomes.

Q: Can hypertension be cured?
A: Primary hypertension cannot be cured but can be effectively managed. Secondary hypertension may resolve if the underlying cause is treated. With proper medication adherence and lifestyle changes, blood pressure can be controlled to safe levels. However, stopping treatment often causes blood pressure to rise again, so most people require long-term management.

Q: How does salt affect blood pressure?
A: Excessive sodium intake causes the body to retain water, increasing blood volume and consequently raising blood pressure. The recommended sodium intake for people with hypertension is less than 1,500 mg per day, while the general recommendation is less than 2,300 mg per day. Reducing sodium can lower blood pressure by 2-8 mmHg. Processed foods, canned goods, and restaurant meals are the major sources of excess sodium in the diet.

Q: What is white coat hypertension?
A: White coat hypertension refers to elevated blood pressure readings that occur only in a medical setting, such as a doctor's office, but not in other settings. It is believed to result from anxiety triggered by the clinical environment. It affects approximately 15-30% of people diagnosed with hypertension. 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring or home monitoring can help distinguish white coat hypertension from true hypertension.
