MEDICAL FAQ - HEART DISEASE

Q: What is heart disease?
A: Heart disease, also called cardiovascular disease, refers to a range of conditions that affect the heart and blood vessels. The most common type is coronary artery disease (CAD), in which plaque builds up in the arteries that supply blood to the heart, reducing or blocking blood flow. Other types include heart failure, arrhythmia, heart valve disease, and congenital heart defects. Heart disease is the leading cause of death globally.

Q: What are the warning signs of a heart attack?
A: Heart attack warning signs include chest pain or discomfort (pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain) that may radiate to the arm, jaw, back, or neck; shortness of breath; cold sweat; nausea or lightheadedness. Women may experience more subtle symptoms such as fatigue, indigestion-like discomfort, or jaw pain. If you suspect a heart attack, call emergency services immediately — time is critical. Do not drive yourself to the hospital.

Q: What causes heart disease?
A: Major risk factors include high blood pressure, high cholesterol, smoking, diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, excessive alcohol use, family history, age, and stress. Atherosclerosis (buildup of plaque in arteries) is the underlying process in most heart disease. Inflammation, poor lifestyle choices, and genetic predisposition all contribute to the development of cardiovascular disease over time.

Q: How is heart disease diagnosed?
A: Diagnosis involves a combination of medical history, physical examination, and diagnostic tests. Common tests include electrocardiogram (ECG/EKG), echocardiogram, stress test, blood tests (cholesterol, cardiac enzymes), chest X-ray, coronary angiography, CT scan, and MRI. Regular screening of blood pressure and cholesterol levels helps detect risk early. Early detection allows for more effective management and prevention of serious complications.

Q: How is heart disease treated?
A: Treatment depends on the type and severity of heart disease. Options include lifestyle changes (diet, exercise, smoking cessation), medications (statins, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, blood thinners), and procedures or surgeries (angioplasty, stent placement, bypass surgery, valve repair or replacement, implantable defibrillators, pacemakers). Cardiac rehabilitation programs help patients recover and reduce future risk after a heart event.

Q: Can heart disease be prevented?
A: Many forms of heart disease can be prevented or significantly reduced through lifestyle modifications. Key prevention strategies include not smoking, maintaining healthy blood pressure and cholesterol, managing diabetes, eating a heart-healthy diet (low in saturated fat, trans fats, and sodium), exercising regularly, maintaining healthy weight, limiting alcohol, managing stress, and getting regular medical checkups. Starting these habits early provides the greatest benefit.

Q: What is cholesterol and how does it affect the heart?
A: Cholesterol is a waxy, fat-like substance found in the blood. LDL (low-density lipoprotein) cholesterol, known as "bad" cholesterol, contributes to plaque buildup in arteries, increasing the risk of heart disease and stroke. HDL (high-density lipoprotein) cholesterol is "good" cholesterol that helps remove LDL from the bloodstream. Triglycerides are another type of blood fat. High total cholesterol, high LDL, and low HDL all increase cardiovascular risk.

Q: What is heart failure?
A: Heart failure is a condition in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs. It can occur when the heart is too weak to pump effectively (systolic failure) or when the heart muscle is stiff and cannot fill properly (diastolic failure). Symptoms include shortness of breath, fatigue, swollen legs and ankles, rapid or irregular heartbeat, and reduced ability to exercise. Despite its name, heart failure does not mean the heart has stopped.

Q: What does an electrocardiogram (ECG) show?
A: An electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) records the electrical activity of the heart. It shows the heart rate and rhythm, and can detect abnormalities such as arrhythmias (irregular heartbeats), heart attacks (past or current), enlarged heart chambers, and problems with the heart's electrical conduction system. It is a painless, non-invasive test performed by attaching electrodes to the skin of the chest, arms, and legs.

Q: What is atrial fibrillation?
A: Atrial fibrillation (AFib) is the most common serious cardiac arrhythmia. In AFib, the upper chambers of the heart (atria) beat chaotically and irregularly instead of coordinating effectively with the lower chambers (ventricles). This can cause symptoms like palpitations, fatigue, shortness of breath, and dizziness. AFib significantly increases the risk of stroke, blood clots, and heart failure. Treatment includes blood thinners, rate control medications, rhythm control medications, and procedures like cardioversion or ablation.
