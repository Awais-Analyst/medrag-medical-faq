MEDICAL FAQ - DIABETES

Q: What is diabetes?
A: Diabetes is a chronic disease that occurs when the pancreas does not produce enough insulin, or when the body cannot effectively use the insulin it produces. Insulin is a hormone that regulates blood sugar. Elevated blood sugar (hyperglycemia) is a common effect of uncontrolled diabetes and over time leads to serious damage to many of the body's systems, especially the nerves and blood vessels.

Q: What are the types of diabetes?
A: There are three main types of diabetes. Type 1 diabetes is a chronic condition in which the pancreas produces little or no insulin. Type 2 diabetes affects how the body processes blood sugar (glucose) and is more common in adults. Gestational diabetes develops in some women during pregnancy and usually goes away after giving birth, though it increases the risk of developing Type 2 diabetes later.

Q: What are the symptoms of diabetes?
A: Common symptoms of diabetes include frequent urination, excessive thirst, unexplained weight loss, extreme hunger, blurry vision, numbness or tingling in hands or feet, fatigue, and very slow healing of cuts or bruises. In Type 1 diabetes, symptoms often appear quickly while in Type 2, they may develop slowly over many years.

Q: How is diabetes diagnosed?
A: Diabetes is diagnosed using blood tests. The fasting blood glucose test measures blood sugar after an 8-hour fast. An A1C test reflects average blood sugar over 2-3 months. A random blood sugar test measures blood glucose at any time. A glucose tolerance test measures blood sugar before and after consuming a glucose drink. Results above normal thresholds confirm a diabetes diagnosis.

Q: How is diabetes treated?
A: Diabetes management depends on the type. Type 1 diabetes requires daily insulin injections or an insulin pump. Type 2 diabetes is often managed with lifestyle changes such as healthy eating and exercise, and may also require oral medications or insulin. All people with diabetes benefit from regular blood sugar monitoring, healthy diet, regular physical activity, and routine medical checkups.

Q: Can diabetes be prevented?
A: Type 2 diabetes can be prevented or delayed with lifestyle changes. Maintaining a healthy weight, eating a balanced diet rich in fiber and low in refined sugars, getting regular physical activity (at least 150 minutes per week), and avoiding smoking significantly reduce the risk. Type 1 diabetes currently cannot be prevented as it is an autoimmune condition.

Q: What complications can diabetes cause?
A: Uncontrolled diabetes can lead to serious complications including heart disease and stroke, kidney disease (nephropathy), eye damage that can lead to blindness (retinopathy), nerve damage (neuropathy), foot problems that may require amputation, and increased susceptibility to infections. Early and consistent management greatly reduces the risk of these complications.

Q: What is the HbA1c test?
A: The HbA1c (Hemoglobin A1c) test is a blood test that measures your average blood sugar levels over the past 2 to 3 months. It shows what percentage of hemoglobin is coated with sugar (glycated). An HbA1c below 5.7% is normal, 5.7-6.4% indicates prediabetes, and 6.5% or above indicates diabetes. For people already diagnosed, the target is generally below 7%.

Q: What foods should a diabetic avoid?
A: People with diabetes should limit or avoid sugary beverages like soda and fruit juice, white bread and refined grains, processed and packaged snacks, full-fat dairy products, fried foods, high-fat meats, and foods with added sugars. Instead, they should choose whole grains, lean proteins, non-starchy vegetables, and healthy fats like those found in nuts and avocados.

Q: What is hypoglycemia?
A: Hypoglycemia, also called low blood sugar, occurs when blood glucose falls below normal levels (usually below 70 mg/dL). It is most common in people taking insulin or certain diabetes medications. Symptoms include shakiness, sweating, rapid heartbeat, hunger, dizziness, confusion, and in severe cases, loss of consciousness. Treatment involves consuming fast-acting carbohydrates like glucose tablets, fruit juice, or regular soda.
