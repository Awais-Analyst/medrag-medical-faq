MEDICAL FAQ - CANCER AWARENESS

Q: What is cancer?
A: Cancer is a group of diseases characterized by the uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells. If the spread is not controlled, it can result in death. Cancer can develop in almost any organ or tissue in the body. It arises from genetic mutations that disrupt normal cell growth regulation. There are more than 100 types of cancer, including breast cancer, lung cancer, colon cancer, skin cancer, leukemia, and lymphoma. Cancer is the second leading cause of death globally, but survival rates are improving with advances in detection and treatment.

Q: What are the common warning signs of cancer?
A: Warning signs include unexplained weight loss, fatigue, fever, pain, skin changes (new or changing moles, yellowing, darkening), changes in bowel or bladder habits, unusual bleeding or discharge, thickening or lump in breast or elsewhere, difficulty swallowing, indigestion that doesn't go away, and a cough or hoarseness that doesn't resolve. These symptoms don't always mean cancer, but should be evaluated by a doctor. Early detection significantly improves treatment outcomes.

Q: What causes cancer?
A: Cancer is caused by genetic mutations that alter how cells grow and divide. These mutations can be inherited or acquired through environmental exposures and lifestyle factors. Known risk factors include tobacco use (the leading cause of preventable cancer deaths), excessive sun exposure (UV radiation), ionizing radiation, certain chemicals and carcinogens, viral infections (HPV, HBV, HCV, EBV), obesity, alcohol use, poor diet, physical inactivity, and older age. Some cancers have a strong hereditary component (e.g., BRCA mutations and breast/ovarian cancer).

Q: How is cancer treated?
A: Cancer treatment depends on the type, stage, and location of cancer, as well as the patient's overall health. Main treatment options include surgery (to remove tumors), radiation therapy (using high-energy rays to kill cancer cells), chemotherapy (drugs that kill cancer cells throughout the body), immunotherapy (using the immune system to fight cancer), targeted therapy (drugs targeting specific molecular changes in cancer cells), and hormone therapy. Many patients receive a combination of treatments. Palliative care addresses symptoms and improves quality of life throughout treatment.

Q: What is cancer screening?
A: Cancer screening involves testing for cancer or pre-cancer in people who show no symptoms. Early detection through screening can significantly improve survival rates. Common screenings include mammograms for breast cancer, Pap smears and HPV tests for cervical cancer, colonoscopy for colorectal cancer, low-dose CT scans for lung cancer (in heavy smokers), and PSA tests for prostate cancer. Screening recommendations vary by age, sex, and risk factors. Regular screening as recommended by healthcare providers is crucial for early detection.

Q: What is chemotherapy and what are its side effects?
A: Chemotherapy uses drugs to kill cancer cells or stop them from dividing. It can be used to cure cancer, control its growth, or relieve symptoms. Chemotherapy is usually given in cycles with rest periods to allow the body to recover. Because it affects rapidly dividing cells throughout the body (not just cancer cells), it can cause side effects including nausea and vomiting, fatigue, hair loss, increased infection risk (low white blood cells), anemia (low red blood cells), easy bruising or bleeding, mouth sores, appetite changes, and nerve damage.

Q: What is breast cancer?
A: Breast cancer is cancer that forms in breast tissue, most commonly in the ducts (ductal carcinoma) or lobules (lobular carcinoma). It is the most common cancer in women globally, though men can also develop it. Risk factors include age, family history, BRCA1/2 gene mutations, hormonal factors, alcohol use, and obesity. Symptoms include a lump in the breast or armpit, changes in breast size or shape, skin changes, nipple discharge, and pain. Treatment includes surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, and targeted therapies.

Q: Can lifestyle changes reduce cancer risk?
A: Yes, many cancer risk factors are modifiable. Not smoking or quitting smoking reduces risk of lung, mouth, throat, esophageal, and other cancers. Maintaining healthy weight, exercising regularly, eating a diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and fiber, limiting alcohol, protecting skin from UV radiation, getting vaccinated against cancer-causing viruses (HPV, HBV), avoiding unnecessary radiation, and getting recommended cancer screenings all significantly reduce cancer risk. Collectively, lifestyle changes could prevent up to one-third of cancer deaths.

Q: What is leukemia?
A: Leukemia is a cancer of blood-forming tissues, primarily the bone marrow and lymphatic system. It results in the production of abnormal white blood cells that cannot function properly and crowd out healthy cells. Types include Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL), Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML), Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL), and Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML). Symptoms include fatigue, frequent infections, fever, easy bruising or bleeding, bone pain, and swollen lymph nodes. Treatment includes chemotherapy, targeted therapy, radiation, and stem cell transplantation.

Q: What is palliative care in cancer treatment?
A: Palliative care is specialized medical care focused on providing relief from pain, symptoms, and the stress of serious illness like cancer. It is provided by a team of doctors, nurses, and specialists who work alongside the primary treatment team. Palliative care addresses physical symptoms (pain, nausea, fatigue), psychological issues (anxiety, depression), social and spiritual needs, and helps patients and families make informed decisions. It can be provided alongside curative treatment at any stage of illness and is not limited to end-of-life care. It consistently improves quality of life.
